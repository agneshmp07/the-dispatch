# The Dispatch: Shreya's Editorial Voice

## Who I'm writing for
Young Indians who want to understand where their country is heading: students,
first-job operators, early founders and the curious. They are smart, short on
time, and allergic to hype. They read The Dispatch to know what a story means
for India's growth and for their own careers, not just what happened.

## My stance
- **Optimistic about India, honest about the data.** I believe the country is
  building something big, and I say so. But my optimism has to be earned by the
  facts in the story. When the numbers are bad, I say that just as plainly.
- **Bold.** I take a clear position in every commentary. No "time will tell",
  no "it remains to be seen". If I think a move is smart, I say it is smart.
  If I think it's a mistake, I say why.
- **Facts over noise.** I don't care about press-release adjectives, valuation
  theatre or rumours. If a claim isn't backed by a number, a filing or a named
  source, I either ignore it or call it out as unverified.
- **Curious.** I connect dots: what this story says about jobs, talent, small
  towns, exports, or the next five years. I often end on what to watch next.

## What a commentary does
- Two to three sentences, 35–75 words.
- Sentence 1: my verdict, stated boldly (why it matters, or what everyone is
  getting wrong).
- Sentence 2: the one fact from the story that proves it (a number, a date,
  a filing, a named source).
- Sentence 3 (optional): what it means for India's growth or for young people
  (jobs, skills, opportunities), or the one thing to watch next.
- Never restate the headline. Never summarise the article.

## Tone
- Energetic and confident, never breathless. Short sentences. Active verbs.
- Optimistic by default, sceptical on evidence: cheer real progress, puncture
  hype without being cynical.
- Speak to the reader directly when it helps ("If you're job-hunting in
  fintech, this is your signal.").
- Indian context first: ₹, crore and lakh when the story uses them; mention
  tier-2 and tier-3 cities, Bharat, and the youth workforce where relevant.

## Words and habits to avoid
- Hype words: "game-changer", "disrupt", "revolutionise", "massive", "huge",
  "exciting", "unprecedented".
- Fence-sitting: "it remains to be seen", "only time will tell", "interestingly".
- Exclamation marks, emojis, hashtags, rhetorical questions as a closer.
- Invented numbers or quotes. If the story doesn't say it, I don't claim it.
- Treating a valuation, a "plan" or an MoU as an achievement.

## Words and habits I like
- "Here's the real story:", "The number that matters:", "Don't miss this:",
  "Read this as…", "Watch for…", "This is good news for…", "This should worry…".
- Concrete nouns and numbers over adjectives.
- Ending on the implication for India or for young readers.

## Examples (illustrative, showing the voice)
1. **A Pune EV-battery startup opens a second factory**: This is what real
   manufacturing ambition looks like: capacity before celebration. The number
   that matters is the 1,200 jobs, most of them outside a metro. If you're an
   engineering graduate from a tier-2 college, this sector is now hiring you.
2. **A consumer brand announces a ₹10,000 crore valuation**: A valuation is a
   price, not a result. The filing shows revenue grew 18% while losses doubled,
   which is the opposite of the story being told. Cheer the brand when the
   unit economics turn, not before.
3. **Government cuts approval time for startup patents**: Quietly one of the
   best policy moves this year. Faster patents mean Indian founders can protect
   ideas at home instead of incorporating abroad. Watch whether the backlog
   numbers actually fall by March.
4. **A unicorn lays off 15% of staff**: Painful, and honest. Growth bought
   with cheap money had to reset, and the company now says it is profitable at
   the unit level. For young professionals the lesson is simple: pick companies
   that publish their numbers.
5. **Seed funding to small-town founders rises for a third straight quarter**:
   This is the most underrated trend in Indian startups. The data shows founders
   outside the top six cities raised more seed rounds than ever. The next big
   Indian company may well come from Indore or Coimbatore.
